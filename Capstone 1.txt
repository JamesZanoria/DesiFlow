ABSTRACT[a]
Digital tools have become a practical necessity for small food businesses seeking to reduce errors and operate more efficiently, yet many still rely on fully manual processes for taking orders, computing bills, and tracking stock. At Desi Zaika Snack House, a small Indian restaurant in Cebu, orders are taken verbally, billing is computed by hand, and ingredient stock is estimated by sight rather than logged, leaving the owner unable to trace a shortage back to the dishes that caused it or to judge which menu items remain profitable once ingredient cost is considered. Addressing this gap called for a system built specifically to the restaurant's scale rather than a generic, enterprise-oriented platform. This study aims to design and develop an integrated web-based self-ordering, point-of-sale, and intelligent inventory management system for Desi Zaika Snack House, combining a customer-facing QR ordering interface, a cashier POS module, a kitchen display, and an administrative dashboard, deployed as a web application on Vercel with WebSocket broadcasting and analytics computation hosted on a separate cloud service. The system automates order placement, payment recording, recipe-level stock deduction, and real-time synchronization across all four user roles through WebSocket broadcasting, while a separate analytics microservice generates demand forecasts, menu-engineering classifications, spoilage-risk scores, and a composite business health score without slowing day-to-day transactions. Development follows an Iterative Waterfall model and underwent black-box testing during implementation; User Acceptance Testing involving the owner, staff, and customers during a pilot deployment at Desi Zaika Snack House is planned to assess the system against functional suitability, reliability, and usability. The system is expected to close the current gap between what is sold and what is tracked, reduce manual order and billing errors, and give the owner data-driven visibility into ingredient consumption and dish profitability that manual record-keeping cannot provide. This is expected to demonstrate that a right-sized, cloud-hosted POS and inventory solution can meaningfully support the operational modernization of small, independent food service businesses.

CHAPTER 1[b]
Introduction
1.1 Rationale of the Study
        The Philippine food service industry has seen consistent growth in recent years, with the sector projected to expand at a compound annual growth rate of 14.52% through 2030, driven by rising consumer demand and increasing adoption of digital ordering platforms (National Tax Research Center, 2023). Despite this growth, many independent food businesses, especially small restaurants and specialty eateries, still operate manually. Orders are taken orally and documented on paper, billing is done by hand, and inventory is kept up to date by periodic physical counting rather than methodical tracking. These practices are not unique to any one business, but their consequences are consistently documented: manual ordering methods cause customers to wait in line before their orders can be taken, which lowers customer satisfaction and limits a restaurant’s capacity to serve customers during peak hours (Abel & Ekabua, 2015).


Desi Zaika Snack House is a small Indian restaurant situated in Cebu, Philippines, offering authentic Indian cuisine to the local community. The company tracks ingredient stock through visual inspection, processes all customer orders manually, computes billing without the use of a point-of-sale system, and bases daily operational decisions, such as how much food to prepare and when to restock ingredients, on experience rather than recorded data, just like many independent businesses of its size. The absence of systematic inventory tracking further compounds these inefficiencies by creating a disconnect between what is sold and what needs to be replenished, resulting in either stock shortages during service or wasteful overstocking, both of which carry direct financial costs. Unlike retail businesses that track products as discrete units, a food service establishment consumes ingredients in variable quantities that depend on what is ordered and in what volume. 


Beyond the transactional and inventory problems, the manual operations of Desi Zaika create a deeper informational gap that prevents the owner from making data-driven operational decisions. The owner cannot decide which dishes are most profitable after ingredient costs, which dishes should be promoted or discontinued, how many portions of each dish to prepare on a given day, or whether the company’s financial health is improving or declining over time without sales records. Restaurateurs can now collect customer behavior data and combine it with operational data to make a variety of strategic and operational decisions because of smart technologies and increased data availability (Roy et al., 2022). The growing adoption of digital payment platforms like GCash, which has surpassed 94 million registered users in the Philippines as of 2024, further underscores the need for small food businesses to modernize their transaction infrastructure to meet current customer expectations (Globe Telecom, 2024).


It is within this context that this study proposes the design and development of an integrated web-based self-ordering, point-of-sale, and intelligent inventory management system for Desi Zaika Snack House that not only addresses the transactional inefficiencies of its current manual operations but also introduces an intelligence layer that delivers data-driven operational insights previously accessible only to larger, better-resourced food service establishments.








1.2 Statement of the Problem
1.2.1 General Objective
                To design and develop an integrated web-based self-ordering, point-of-sale, and intelligent inventory management system for Desi Zaika Snack House.
1.2.2 Specific Objectives
1. To analyze the current ordering, production, and inventory processes of Desi Zaika Snack House in order to identify existing operational inefficiencies and define the corresponding system requirements.
2. To design and develop a web-based system that integrates self-ordering, point-of-sale (POS), and kitchen production management into a single platform.
3. To design and develop an inventory module that tracks usage and stock levels in real time.
4. To design and develop a data analytics dashboard that provides the owner with insights into daily business operations.
5. To test and evaluate the usability and effectiveness of the developed system through user acceptance testing and pilot deployment of Desi Zaika Snack House.
1.3 Significance of the Study
        The development of an integrated web-based self-ordering, point-of-sale, and intelligent inventory management system carries meaningful implications for several groups directly and indirectly connected to the operations of Desi Zaika Snack House. The following identifies the primary beneficiaries of this study and the specific ways in which each stands to gain from its outcomes.


        Desi Zaika Snack House. As the primary beneficiary and client of this study, Desi Zaika Snack House stands to gain the most direct and immediate benefit from the system’s development. The business currently relies entirely on manual processes for order-taking, billing, inventory tracking, and operational decision-making, practices that expose it to order errors, ingredient shortages, unquantified food waste, and an inability to assess its own financial performance in any systematic way. The system addresses each of these pain points through a unified platform that automates transactional workflows, tracks ingredients at the recipe level, and surfaces data-driven insights about menu performance, ingredient consumption, and overall business health. The owner and staff of Desi Zaika Snack House are expected to benefit from reduced operational errors, better-informed restocking decisions, clearer visibility into which dishes are most and least profitable, and a daily summary of business health that requires no technical expertise to read or act on.


        Customers of Desi Zaika Snack House. Customers benefit from the introduction of  QR-based self-ordering on their own devices, which allows them to browse the full menu, customize their selections, and place orders at their own pace without waiting for a staff member to become available. The real-time menu availability engine ensures that customers are never presented with dishes that cannot be prepared due to insufficient ingredients, eliminating the frustration of placing an order that must later be cancelled or substituted. The addition of GCash as a payment option alongside cash further accommodates the payment preferences of customers who have already adopted digital wallets in their daily lives, making the transaction experience more convenient and consistent with current consumer habits in the Philippines.


        Kitchen and Service Staff. Staff members responsible for food preparation benefit from the Kitchen Display System, which replaces the current reliance on verbal instructions and handwritten tickets with a live, organized queue of confirmed orders visible directly in the kitchen. This reduces the likelihood of missed or miscommunicated orders during busy service periods and gives kitchen staff a clearer, more manageable view of what needs to be prepared and in what sequence. Cashiers benefit from the point-of-sale module, which streamlines the payment process, automatically computes change for cash transactions, records GCash reference numbers, and generates receipts without requiring manual calculation or paperwork.


        Future Researchers. This study contributes to the growing body of literature on the application of intelligent systems in small-scale food service operations in the Philippines. The integration of a dynamic recipe-ingredient model with real-time inventory management, the implementation of a Menu Engineering Matrix as a computational feature within a POS system, the design of a Business Health Score as a composite operational indicator, and the development of a bounded conversational interface for business querying represent approaches that have not been extensively documented in the context of small independent Philippine restaurants. Future researchers working on similar problems, particularly those involving the digitalization of micro and small enterprises in the food service sector, may draw on the design decisions, methodology, and evaluation findings of this study as a reference point for their own work.


The Philippine Micro and Small Enterprise Sector. At the broadcast level, this study addresses a gap that affects not only Desi Zaika Snack House but a large number of similarly sized food businesses across the country that operate without digital systems and lack access to the kind of operational intelligence that larger restaurant chains take for granted. While the system developed in this study is scoped specifically to a single client, the design principles, architecture, and feature set have been developed with generalizability in mind. The outcomes of this study may inform future efforts to extend similar systems to other micro and small food enterprises in Cebu and across the Philippines, contributing to the broader goal of making data-driven operations accessible to businesses that have historically been excluded from the benefits of digital transformation.
1.4 Scope and Limitations
The scope of this study lies exclusively to the design and development of a web-based integrated food service management system for Desi Zaika Snack House, an Indian restaurant operating in Cebu, Philippines. The operational needs of this specific business define and bound the work undertaken here; the findings and outputs of this study are not intended to generalize across the broader food service industry, nor are they meant to represent an advancement in point-of-sale (POS) or inventory management technology beyond what a small restaurant of this nature requires. Every decision made throughout the development was guided by one governing constraint: the system must remain within the capacity of a four-member student team to design, build, and evaluate within the duration of the capstone engagement, given the financial and time limitations under which the researchers worked. 


The operational layer covers the functions the business needs to run day by day: a web-based self-ordering interface that customers access by scanning a table QR code, a point-of-sale interface through which cashiers process transactions and collect cash or GCash payment, a Kitchen Display System through which kitchen staff receive and manage confirmed orders, and a recipe-based inventory module that tracks ingredient consumption at the dish level. 


The analytics layer covers a data analytics dashboard that turns the records the operational layer produces into insights that support the owner’s daily business decisions. This layer is scoped to what the owner needs on a day-to-day basis; it is not intended to expand beyond the need.


Several boundaries apply to what the system covers. It is built and tested for one location only; questions of multi-branch inventory coordination or consolidated reporting across sites are not addressed. While cash and GCash are both accepted at the counter, the system does not process payments online, support order-ahead transactions, or connect to any digital wallet other than GCash. Discounts, promotional pricing, and voucher redemption are not part of the ordering or payment workflow. When a void or refund becomes necessary, the cashier submits a request through the system, and the transaction is only reversed after the admin or manager reviews and approves the request. The system is a web application and is not packaged as a native app for any mobile platform. The Laravel web application was deployed on Vercel, while Laravel Reverb (real-time WebSocket broadcasting) and the FastAPI analytics microservice were deployed on a separate cloud host (Render) capable of running them as persistent processes, a deliberate choice given the single-location scale of the business, the team’s limited budget, and the absence of any need for on-premise infrastructure at this stage.


        The system’s limitations included the following. First, it required compatible hardware, such as a desktop or tablet device to run the POS interface, to function optimally; any malfunction or incompatibility with this device could disrupt sales and kitchen operations. Second, because the web application, the WebSocket broadcasting layer, and the analytics microservice are hosted on external cloud platforms rather than an on-site server, the restaurant’s own internet connection became a hard dependency: unlike a purely local-network deployment, none of the four modules can function if the restaurant’s internet access goes down, a trade-off accepted in exchange for the reliability, automatic scaling, and remote accessibility a managed cloud host provides over a single on-site machine. Third, since Desi Zaika Snack House is only beginning to keep digital records, the analytics layer will initially operate on limited or seeded data, and its accuracy is expected to develop gradually as the system accumulates real transactional history. Lastly, employees needed to undergo training to transition from manual operations to the digital system, as the client noted they previously lacked proficiency in technologies beyond their day-to-day tools.

CHAPTER 2
Related Systems


        This chapter presents a review literature and existing systems relevant to the proposed study. It examines prior research on the key concepts, technologies, and system implementations that relate to the design and development of an integrated web-based self-ordering, point-of-sale, and intelligent inventory management system for Desi Zaika Snack House. 
2.1 Review of Related Literature
Manual and Automation in Data Entry
Data entry is the process of transferring information from a source document into a computer system so that it can be stored, retrieved, and processed. This task is typically done manually with a person putting information directly into a spreadsheet. While this method is flexible and only costs minimal special equipment, it has its flaws; this process is prone to human error. According to Wynn (2021), a manual inventory management system has an increased risk of human error; it may result in misplacement and waste, which can be costly. Automating the inventory system especially with a POS system can make this process more efficient and remove any human errors. It can solve problems like data synchronization and can make for faster decision making. Furthermore, studies have shown that by adopting automated data entry these businesses reduce the processing time and costs. Automated systems help and enable companies to optimize efficiency, improve on accuracy, and allocate resources for health and longevity of the business.


Inventory Management System
In today’s business world, effective stock inventory management has become critical (Madamidola et al., 2017). Globally, inventory managers confront the challenges of maintaining continuous production processes, delivering quality services to consumers, managing optimal stock levels, and implementing effective inventory management policies (Madamidola et al., 2017). As a business grows, numerous problems also arise. First, official record-keeping will need to change because managers will find it difficult to keep track of the numerous items that need to be kept in the inventory. In addition, more room is needed to do business as inventory amounts increase (Madamidola et al., 2017).


Inventory management is one of the most complex functions of an organization due to the multiple uncertainties that surround its environment (Vidal et al., 2019). According to their state during processing, inventories can be divided into four categories: (1) raw materials, which comprise the fundamental components used in production; (2) work-in-process inventories, which are waiting between operations after direct labor and indirect costs have been applied; (3) finished products, which are completed and ready for customer delivery; and (4) materials and supplies, which include consumer goods like spare parts for equipment maintenance (Vidal et al., 2019).


Web-based Ordering and QR Code Systems
The development of information technology has encouraged companies to adapt to improve operational effectiveness and efficiency, particularly in inventory management (Mandalangi et al., 2025). Web-based ordering is the use of Internet technologies to replace or enhance traditional manual and phone-based ordering methods by allowing customers and businesses to place, process, and track orders via a web platform. Web-based systems for inventory control are very important for the efficiency of the areas they are present in small, medium, and large organizations, where a change is reflected through the use of web technologies (Misahuaman et al., 2021).


Following the COVID-19 pandemic, when contactless interactions became a health and safety priority, QR code-based ordering, a particular application of web-based ordering, became more and more common in the food service sector. A unique code is placed on or at each table in a QR code ordering system. When a customer scans the code with their own device, they are taken to a web-based menu where they can browse, customize, and submit their order without having to download any apps or deal with employees. Tharun et al. (2023) discovered that the method increases order efficiency by enabling patrons to place orders straight from their devices, lessening the workload for employees, and doing away with the requirement for specialized hardware at each table.


Point of Sale (POS) System
Data inconsistencies, human error, and operational inefficiencies are common problems for small and medium-sized businesses (SMEs) due to disjointed and unintegrated systems (Landge et al., 2026). The demand for effective and dependable POS and inventory management systems has increased due to the growing digitalization of retail operations (Landge et al., 2026).


Kitchen Display Systems
In most small establishments, communication between the cashier and kitchen still relies on verbal relay or handwritten order tickets, practices that are vulnerable to miscommunication and loss when service volume increases. Panneerselvam et al. (2022) noted that an integrated restaurant management system must include a kitchen management component to handle food production and order routing efficiently, arguing that POS, order management, and kitchen management cannot function optimally as isolated components. Mohd Zaini et al. (2023) described an integrated restaurant ordering system as a combination of distinct subsystems designed to streamline the process of accepting and managing orders, with a kitchen module serving as the back-of-house counterpart to the front-of-house POS.


The value of real-time order information sharing in kitchen operations was established by Miyaura et al. (2016), who introduced a real-time process management system for restaurants in which kitchen staff could check all customer orders by dish and elapsed time, confirming that such systems make preparation processes more efficient and reduce service errors and customer complaints. Rahul et al. (2024) proposed the integration of a POS system, Kitchen Display System, and automated inventory management for a small noodle eatery in Indonesia and found that the combination significantly improved service speed, order accuracy, and stock efficiency, and that the KDS component specifically eliminated kitchen workflow bottlenecks that had previously caused order duplication and preparation delays.


Customer QR-based Self-Ordering
Self-service technologies designed to improve service quality and customer experience have gained significant adoption in food service, replacing or supplementing traditional interactions between service providers and customers (Yaacob et al., 2021). QR code-based table ordering represents one of the most practically accessible implementations of this technology, as it requires no dedicated hardware installation and allows customers to use their own familiar devices to place orders.


Attributes influencing customers’ continuous usage of automated self-service technology in fast-food restaurants was investigated, finding that system quality, innovativeness, safety, and security significantly influence continuous usage intention (Hashim et al., 2023). Customers are more likely to continuously operate a self-service ordering kiosk if they think it is more advanced than traditional ordering methods (Hashim et al., 2023). Lantican (2025) studied customer satisfaction on the use of self-service technologies in selected fast-food restaurants, finding that self-service kiosks offer fast, convenient ordering and empower customers, but payment issues can disrupt the experience and require better system design.


Alfaren and Arijanto (2021) studied a self-ordering concept food ordering system in restaurants and found that such systems can effectively assist restaurant operations in accepting orders, making it easier for customers to place orders for items they want without requiring staff availability at the moment of ordering. Their work demonstrated that the self-ordering concept is applicable across a range of restaurant contexts, including smaller establishments where staffing is limited.


Recipe-Based Inventory Management
Unlike retail businesses that track products as discrete units, food service establishments consume ingredients in variable quantities that depend on what is ordered. Sukorra et al. (2021) applied the Bill of Materials framework specifically to food menu analysis and found that formalizing the ingredient composition of each dish before production increases transparency in planning and reduces uncertainty in both product composition and material usage tracking. Naumov (2023) established that standardization in menu item recipes and portioning adds directly to financial control in restaurant operations, while Siek and Guswanto (2021) demonstrated that an effective food service inventory system must minimize losses from both spoilage due to overstocking and customer dissatisfaction due to understocking, a balance achievable only through ingredient-level tracking rather than product-level sales recording.




Food Waste and Spoilage Management
Food waste is a significant and well-documented problem in food service operations, with consequences that are both financial and operational for small businesses on thin margins. Dhir et al. (2020) conducted a systematic literature review on food waste in hospitality and food service operations and found that poor inventory management and inadequate tracking of perishable stock are among the most consistently identified drivers of kitchen-level waste. The United Nations Environment Programme (2021) estimated that food service establishments account for approximately 26% of total food waste globally.


The management of perishable and non-perishable requires differentiated monitoring because their deterioration rates differ substantially. Siek and Guswanto (2021) found that restaurants managing mixed perishable and non-perishable inventories benefit from systems that apply category-specific monitoring frequencies. Rossi (2021) described predictive analytics applied to inventory management as enabling organizations to move from reactive to proactive stock management by connecting shelf life data to observed consumption patterns.


Data Analytics 
In inventory management, data analytics refers to the application of statistical, machine learning, and business intelligence approaches to estimate demand, optimize stock levels, evaluate inventory data, and facilitate data-driven decision-making. Traditional inventory management approaches suffer from manual errors, lack of real-time updates, and restricted reporting capabilities (S & P, 2026). The integration of data analytics into inventory management has proven to be one of the most critical aspects concerning optimization, streamlining inventory management, order fulfillment, and operational efficiency (Mulla, 2022).


Rossi (2021) described inventory analytics as a comprehensive discipline covering the foundations of inventory systems, prescriptive analytics models for deterministic inventory control, predictive analytics techniques for demand forecasting, and prescriptive analytics models for stochastic inventory control. Roy et al. (2022) noted that smart technologies and increased data availability enable restaurateurs to gather information about customer behavior and operational performance and that this data can be combined across sources to support a wide range of strategic and operational decisions. A critical gap in the existing literature, however, is the near-complete absence of analytics implementations designed for small independent food businesses in the Philippine context.


Menu Engineering
The systematic evaluation of restaurant menu performance has been a subject of hospitality management research since the early 1980s, with the Menu Engineering Model developed by Kasavana and Smith (1982) remaining the most widely cited framework in both academic research and practice. Menu engineering evaluates individual menu item performance according to sales volume and contribution margin, with the purpose of identifying which dishes sell well and yield high profits and which do not, so that management can plan targeted actions to address underperforming items (Lee et al., 2004).


Mutlu et al. (2022) studied the application of menu engineering among kitchen chefs in restaurants and found that while chefs generally have an intuitive sense of which dishes perform well, they rely on qualitative judgement rather than structured quantitative analysis, a gap that menu engineering closes by replacing intuition with a reproducible, data-grounded classifications. Horváth et al. (2022) examined the Kasavana and Smith model in a restaurant context and found that the matrix methodology effectively identifies the most profitable items and those requiring methodology effectively identifies the most profitable items and those requiring modification. Lee et al. (2004) applied menu engineering specifically to Asian restaurants and found that many items analyzed were classified as unpopular or unprofitable despite being retained on the menu based on cultural or variety considerations, suggesting that the framework is particularly valuable for specialty restaurants like Indian eateries where intuition-based menu decisions may not align with financial reality. No existing study has implemented a computationally automated Menu Engineering Matrix as a built-in feature of a small business POS system in the Philippine context.


Digital Payments and GCash
The adoption of digital payment methods in the Philippines has accelerated significantly in recent years. Acopiado et al. (2022) studied digital payment adoption during the COVID-19 pandemic in the Philippines and found that the crisis catalyzed a shift toward cashless transactions, with mobile wallets becoming significantly more prominent in everyday purchasing behavior. As of 2023, GCash had surpassed 60 million registered users nationwide, establishing itself as the leading digital wallet platform in the country (Globe, as cited in Ong et al., 2024).


Cacas et al. (2022) investigated factors influencing mobile wallet adoption in the Philippines and found that perceived ease of use, social influence, and merchant availability are significant determinants of adoption intention. Afable (2024) examined GCash as a digital payment innovation and found that it has emerged as a dependable financial tool for Filipinos, significantly altering daily financial routines across income groups, with its localized ecosystems providing a distinct competitive advantage over international platforms.




Smart Assistant
A smart assistant in the context of a restaurant inventory management system is an artificial intelligence (AI)-driven software agent that combines conversational capabilities with data analytics to perform operational tasks, such as taking orders and managing ingredients, while simultaneously analyzing business data to generate daily, automated insights and actionable recommendations for the owner. Rafailidis and Manolopoulos (2019) observed that virtual assistants, also known as intelligent conversational systems, interact with human-like responses to users’ queries and finish specific tasks, and that the gap between virtual assistants and recommendation systems is narrowing as both converge toward personalized, conversational decision support.


Automated Daily Business Insights and Decision Support
The distinguished feature of a smart assistant for small restaurant owners is its capacity to analyze daily operational data and proactively deliver actionable insights without being explicitly queried. This capability through a scenario in which a small coffee shop owner receives a daily notification from her AI-powered Small Business Insights dashboard reminding her to order supplies; the system analyzes her past buying behavior, scans for the best deals online, updates real-time cash flow projections, alerts her to scheduling conflicts and potential loan needs, and even provides weekly industry updates, all without the owner having to run a single report (Mills & Dang, 2021). This vision of an always-on, proactive business partner aligns directly with the concept of the smart assistant feature in the present study. The practical impact of AI and intelligent personal assistants (IPAs) was analyzed on business processes in small and medium-sized service enterprises, identifying concrete use cases and evaluating them with experts; their results showed that IPA features in particular are perceived useful, with AI and IPA features having a cost savings potential of over 31% on average (Husson et al., 2021).


Real-Time Systems and WebSocket Communication
The operational demands of a multi-role food service require that order status, ingredient availability, and transaction information be shared across all connected parties simultaneously and without delay. Miyaura et al. (2016) established that sharing order information in real time across kitchen and front-of-house staff is a fundamental requirement for making restaurant preparation processes more efficient. Alt (2021) established that digital transformation in restaurant operations now encompasses both ordering and kitchen management functions, with POS systems designed to capture orders and forward them to the kitchen for task scheduling while simultaneously updating inventory records.


WebSocket technology enables this real-time communication by establishing a persistent, bidirectional connection between a server and its connected clients. Fonseka and Fonseka (2012) found that the WebSocket protocol significantly reduces latency compared to HTTP polling approaches and enables a more responsive user experience in applications where multiple parties need to see the same data update simultaneously. Gill (2022) identified WebSocket-based real-time processing as a critical architecture component for restaurant online ordering systems, noting that event-driven communication is necessary to keep inventory records, kitchen displays, and customer status screens synchronized under concurrent order flows from multiple channels. Sharma et al. (2025) confirmed that WebSocket-based real-time order tracking offers customers transparency and assurance, contributing to increased satisfaction.


2.2 Review of Related Systems
This section examines existing commercial systems whose features relate directly to the system proposed for Desi Zaika Snack House. Each system is described individually, identifying what it accomplishes, which of its features this study intends to adopt or adapt, and where it falls short of what a small, single-location Philippine restaurant requires.


MarketMan. MarketMan is a cloud-based restaurant back-of-house platform that centralizes inventory tracking, purchasing, recipe costing, and AI-assisted demand-based ordering, allowing operations to breaSk recipes down to ingredient-level cost and generate suggested reorder quantities from historical usage and vendor data (MarketMan, 2026). This study replicates MarketMan’s core idea that ingredient-level recipe costing should update automatically as purchasing and sales data change. MarketMan is explicitly positioned as a back-of-house tool rather than an all-in-one restaurant platform, and its own documentation describes it as designed to integrate with a restaurant's existing POS system rather than replace one; it therefore does not include customer-facing ordering, kitchen display, or menu profitability classification, consistent with its stated scope as a purchasing and inventory add-on. The proposed system differs by combining an equivalent recipe-costing layer with ordering, payment, and kitchen coordination inside a single platform, which suits the single-location scale of Desi Zaika Snack House.


Craftable. Craftable connects purchase orders, invoice data, physical counts, and live vendor pricing to recipe costs, allowing operators to see theoretical versus actual food cost variance in real time and providing a menu engineering feature that classifies dishes by profitability (Craftable, 2026). This study adopts Craftable's approach of tying purchasing and recipe data together so that ingredient price changes flow automatically into dish-level cost and profitability classification. Craftable markets itself toward multi-location hospitality groups and bar programs and explicitly integrates with external POS platforms such as Square rather than providing its own; ordering, kitchen display, and forecasting are outside its stated scope. The proposed system is scoped differently, toward a single small restaurant needing these functions built-in rather than integrated from separate vendors, which better fits the budget and operational simplicity.


Klikit. Klikit provides self-service ordering kiosks and QR-based digital ordering for restaurants, with AI-driven upselling and a unified dashboard that consolidates orders from multiple channels (Klikit, 2026). This study adopts Klikit's underlying self-ordering interaction model, letting customers browse and submit orders without staff involvement, adapting it here to a QR-to-personal-device flow rather than dedicated kiosk hardware. Klikit's documentation focuses on order consolidation and upselling and does not describe a recipe-based inventory component, which is consistent with its positioning as an ordering and delivery-aggregation tool rather than an inventory system. The proposed system extends this ordering model by connecting it directly to a real-time menu availability engine, a function outside what Klikit sets out to provide.


Square (Square for Restaurants). Square for Restaurants combines point-of-sale payment processing with restaurant-specific tools including table management, a Kitchen Display System, and a real-time inventory sync across sales channels (NerdWallet, 2026; Square, 2026). This study adopts Square's approach of pairing POS transactions directly with kitchen order routing and stock-level tracking in one platform. Independent reviews note that Square's inventory tracking operates at the product level and that deeper recipe costing is only available through a third-party add-on rather than natively (NerdWallet, 2026), and its reporting is described as covering standard sales and shift performance rather than menu profitability classification or demand forecasting. Since Square markets itself as covering restaurant operations broadly, this is a closer functional gap rather than simply a difference in scope; the proposed system addresses it by building recipe-level tracking and menu engineering directly into the same platform that handles POS and kitchen display.


Restroworks. Restroworks offers a Kitchen Display System, recipe-based inventory management, and demand forecasting, aimed primarily at multi-location restaurant chains coordinating a central kitchen with several outlets (Restroworks, 2026). This study adopts Restroworks' integration of recipe costing with POS-synced inventory deduction and its use of forecasting to support purchasing, both reflected in this study's Bill of Materials model and reorder recommendation feature. Restroworks' own materials describe it as built for multi-brand, multi-location operations, which is a difference in target scale rather than a shortcoming; a platform built for that scale is reasonably expected to carry complexity and pricing disproportionate to a single independent restaurant. The proposed system is scoped instead for one location, matching Desi Zaika Snack House's actual operational size.


Meez. Meez is a recipe management and food-costing platform that connects live purchasing data to recipe costs and classifies menu items by food cost percentage, profit margin, and sales volume (Meez, 2026). This is the closest existing parallel to this study's Menu Engineering Matrix, and this study adopts meez's approach of recalculating dish profitability automatically as costs and sales data change. Meez is explicitly positioned as a recipe and costing tool that imports sales data from an external POS rather than generating it directly, so the absence of ordering, POS, or kitchen display functionality reflects its stated scope rather than an omission. The proposed system generates its own sales data internally by combining ordering, POS, and kitchen coordination with the same underlying profitability logic.


Synthesis. These six systems show a consistent market pattern rather than a set of individual shortcomings: back-of-house tools (MarketMan, Craftable, Meez) are generally built to integrate with a separate POS rather than replace one, while front-of-house tools (Klikit, Square) generally treat recipe-level costing and menu analytics as outside their core focus. Restroworks is the one system spanning both categories, but is scoped for multi-location chains rather than single, independent restaurants. This is a structural feature of how the restaurant software market is currently segmented, rather than a flaw specific to any one product. Two features were not found in the documentation of any of the six systems reviewed: a real-time menu availability engine that automatically removes a dish from the customer-facing ordering interface the moment its ingredients fall below preparation threshold, and a composite daily business health indicator that consolidates revenue, inventory, menu profitability, and waste into a single interpretable figure. Their absence across all six systems may reflect that these features address a need specific to small, single-location, resource-constrained restaurants rather than the broader commercial market these products are built for. This is the segment the proposed system is designed to serve, combining ordering, POS, kitchen display, recipe-based inventory, and analytics into one platform built specifically for that scale, rather than assembled from several specialized products.

CHAPTER 3[c]
Technical Background


        This chapter discusses the foundational technologies and tools utilized in the development of an integrated web-based self-ordering, point-of-sale, and intelligent inventory management system for Desi Zaika Snack House.


Database Management System
        The system used PostgreSQL as its relational database management system (DBMS) to allow authorized users and system processes to perform create, read, update, and delete (CRUD) operations on all business data, including menu items, recipes, ingredient stock, orders, and transactions. PostgreSQL is a powerful, open-source object-relational database system with over 35 years of active development, known for its robustness, extensibility, and strict adherence to SQL standards (PostgreSQL Global Development Group, 2024). It enforces data integrity through primary keys, foreign keys, and referential constraints, properties essential for a multi-module system where orders, ingredients, recipes, and transactions must remain consistently linked. A comparative study found that PostgreSQL demonstrates superior handling of concurrent read and write operations compared to other open-source relational databases, making it suitable for a multi-role system where cashiers, kitchen staff, administrators, and the analytics microservice may simultaneously access the database (Klimek & Skublewska-Paszkowska, 2024). Its relational structure ensures that the RECIPE_INGREDIENTS mapping and stock movement ledger remain accurate and auditable at all times.


Application Deployment
        The system was deployed as a split cloud architecture rather than a single on-site server. The Laravel web application, covering the Admin Dashboard, POS, Kitchen Display System, and self-ordering interface, was deployed on Vercel, while Laravel Reverb (the WebSocket broadcasting layer) and the FastAPI analytics microservice were deployed on a separate host (Render) capable of keeping those two components running continuously, something Vercel’s serverless execution model does not support. This split was necessary because Vercel executes code in short-lived, stateless functions rather than a persistent process, whereas Reverb must hold WebSocket connections open indefinitely and the analytics microservice’s scheduler must remain in memory to run on a recurring schedule. This approach requires a stable restaurant internet connection for the system to function at all, unlike a purely on-site deployment, but in exchange gives the system the reliability, automatic scaling, and off-site accessibility of managed cloud hosting. Both Vercel and Render offer free or low-cost tiers suitable for a single-location deployment of this scale, keeping recurring hosting costs modest relative to the team’s ₱8,000 budget.


Model-View-Controller Architecture
        The system followed the Model-View-Controller (MVC) architectural pattern, which separates an application into three components: The Model managing data and business logic, the View handling what the user sees, and the Controller mediating between the two. This separation improves maintainability, testability, and scalability by ensuring changes to one layer do not unnecessarily affect the others (Huynh et al., 2022). For the system, MVC is critical because the system serves four distinct user roles such as the admin, cashier, kitchen staff, and customer, each requiring different view layers and access-controlled controller logic built on the same underlying data models.


Backend Framework
        The transactional backend was built using Laravel, a modern open-source PHP framework that implements the MVC pattern and provides built-in features including database migration management, Eloquent ORM, role-based authentication middleware, task scheduling, and event broadcasting (Subecz, 2021). Huynh et al. (2022) demonstrated that Laravel’s architecture produces standardized, scalable development workflows that significantly reduce unexpected errors common in traditional PHP development. Laravel manages all transactional functions in the system such as the authentication, role-based access, ordering flow, POS processing, inventory management, and analytics job scheduling.


Analytics Microservice
        Python and FastAPI were used to create a specialized analytics microservice that manages all statistical calculations needed by the intelligence layer. According to performance evaluations, FastAPI, a high-performance Python web framework for asynchronous API development, has an average response time of 95 milliseconds under moderate load (Mabotha et al., 2024). Python was chosen because it provides a mature ecosystem of statistical libraries such as pandas, statsmodels, and scikit-learn which are not available in PHP. The microservice reads from the shared PostgreSQL database using read-only credentials, runs its computations, and writes results to an analytics snapshots table that the Laravel application reads to populate the analyst dashboard. This two-service architecture ensures heavy batch processing does not affect the responsiveness of other modules.


Frontend Styling
        The system’s user interfaces were styled using Tailwind CSS, a utility-first CSS framework that enables developers to build responsive layouts by composing pre-defined utility classes directly in the markup. Al Salmi (2023) described Tailwind CSS as promoting a mobile-first design philosophy that ensures interface consistency across varying screen sizes, a property directly relevant to the proposed system where the ordering interface must work on customer smartphones, the POS on a counter tablet, and the kitchen display on a wall-mounted screen. Tailwind was chosen over component-based frameworks because it integrates cleanly with Laravel Blade templates and imposes no Javascript component overhead.






Real-Time Communication
        Real-time synchronization across all modules was implemented using WebSocket-based event broadcasting through Laravel Echo paired with Laravel Reverb as the self-hosted broadcast transport layer, deployed on Render alongside the analytics microservice rather than on Vercel, since maintaining an open WebSocket connection requires a persistent process that Vercel’s serverless functions cannot provide. WebSockets establish a persistent, bidirectional connection between the server and connected clients, allowing the server to push updates the moment a relevant event occurs without any page refresh. Fonseka and Fonseka (2012) confirmed that WebSocket communication significantly reduces latency compared to HTTP polling approaches in multi-user applications. Laravel Reverb was chosen over third-party services such as Pusher because it is self-hosted and incurs no per-message cost.


Internet Dependency and Remote Access Considerations
The system is hosted on Vercel and Render rather than a single on-site device, an individual machine failing no longer takes the POS, Kitchen Display System, self-ordering interface, or Admin Dashboard offline, both platforms manage the underlying server infrastructure and restart failed processes automatically. This is a deliberate trade-off in the opposite direction from a purely local deployment: cloud hosting introduces a dependency on the restaurant’s internet connection that a local-network-only design would not have, since none of the four modules can be reached if that connection drops, but it removes the single-device failure risk and lets the owner access the Admin Dashboard remotely from outside the restaurant, a capability a purely on-site deployment would not normally provide without a VPN.




Data Visualization
        Scales and analytics data were visualized using Chart.js , an open-source JavaScript library that renders interactive charts on HTML5 canvas elements client-side, keeping chart generation fast and independent of backend load (Chart.js, 2023). In the proposed system, Chart.js renders revenue trend charts on the Sales Report page, profit margin visualizations on the Analytics Report page, and the Business Health Score trend line. It was selected for its straightforward integration with Laravel Blade, broad chart type support, and zero licensing cost.


Version Control
        The development of the system was managed using Git for version control and Github as the remote repository hosting platform. Git is a distributed version control system that allows multiple developers to work on different features simultaneously through branching and merging without overwriting each other’s contributions (Loeliger & McCullough, 2012). Github provides the team with a centralized remote repository, pull request management for code review, and an issue tracker for logging bugs and tasks identified during testing.


UI/UX Design
        All user interface wireframes and interaction prototypes were designed using Figma, a cloud-based collaborative design tool that allows multiple designers to work on the same file simultaneously and share interactive prototypes with stakeholders without requiring software installation (Figma, 2024). Figma was used to produce high-fidelity wireframes for all four modules before implementation began, ensuring the team agreed on layout and navigation flow before writing any code and allowing the client to preview and approve the interface design early in development.






Integrated Development Environment (IDE)
        The system was developed using Visual Studio Code (VS Code), a free, open-source code editor by Microsoft that supports PHP, Python, Javascript, and SQL, making it suitable for a multi-language project like our proposed system (Microsoft, 2024). Key extensions used include PHP Intelephense, the Laravel Artisan extension, the Python extension, Tailwind CSS IntelliSense, and SQLTools for querying PostgreSQL directly from the editor. Ardito (2021) noted that VS Code’s combination of lightweight performance and extensible language support makes it one of the most widely adopted editors for modern web development, a finding consistent with Stack Overflow’s developer surveys which have ranked it as the most used development environment globally for multiple consecutive years (Stack Overflow, 2023).


Local Development Environment
        Local development and testing of the Laravel application was conducted using XAMPP, a free cross-platform web server stack providing Apache and PHP in a single installation. XAMPP allows the team to run the full application locally without requiring an active internet connection during development, enabling faster iteration before pushing changes to the production server (Apache Friends, 2024). A local PostgreSQL instance is used alongside XAMPP for database management during development.


QR Code-Based Access
        Customer access to the self-ordering interface was provided through printed QR codes placed at each table. Scanning the code directs the customer’s personal device to the web-based ordering interface without requiring any application download. Research confirmed that QR code-based ordering improves order efficiency by allowing customers to place orders directly from their own devices, reducing the interaction burden on staff and eliminating the need for dedicated hardware (Tharun et al., 2023). Each QR code initiates a table session that persists until the customer closes the interface or rescans the code.


Authentication and Security
        User authentication and access control were managed through Laravel’s built-in authentication system using role-based middleware that restricts each user to functions appropriate for their assigned role. All passwords are stored using bcrypt hashing, all communication occurs over HTTPS, and all form submissions are protected by CSRF token verification. Al-Aqrabi et al. (2019) established that combining role-based access control with encrypted transmission and secure credential storage represents the appropriate security baseline for web-based applications handling sensitive business and financial data.
































CHAPTER 4
DESIGN AND METHODOLOGY


This chapter presents the research design adopted for the development of the Integrated Self-Ordering and POS System with Real-Time Inventory Management for Desi Zaika Snack House, the requirement specifications that guided its construction, the hardware and software requirements necessary for its operation, and the architecture of the resulting system.


4.1 Conceptual Framework
        As shown in Figure 1, the current business process at Desi Zaika Snack House begins when a customer arrives and places an order directly with the cashier. The cashier writes the order down and computes the total by hand, since no point-of-sale terminal is in use. Payment is accepted in cash or through GCash, with the cashier visually confirming the GCash payment screen shown by the customer rather than verifying it though any payment gateway. Once payment is settled, the cashier relays the order to the kitchen staff verbally, and the kitchen staff prepare the dish from memory of what was said. When the order is ready, it is served directly to the customer, and the transaction ends there with no digital record created at any step.


        Ingredient stock is handled the same way: on no fixed schedule, the owner or a staff member walks through the kitchen and estimates what is left by sight, without weighing, counting, or logging the result anywhere. Because this check happens independently of the ordering and cooking sequence, there is no way to trace a short  age back to which dishes consumed the ingredient, or to know how close to running out an item actually is until it visibly runs out. This gap between what is sold and what is tracked is the central inefficiency the proposed system is built to close.




Figure 1. Current Business Process Flowchart 
  









Figure 2. Conceptual Framework
  



        The conceptual framework of this study, illustrated in Figure 2, presents a narrative walkthrough of how a single customer transaction moves through the proposed system, from the moment an order is placed to the moment that same transaction feeds into the business intelligence the owner reviews. Each icon in the diagram represents a discrete stage of this process, and the arrows connecting them represent the sequence in which the four user roles, Customer, Cashier, Kitchen Staff, and Admin, interact with the system to complete it.


The process begins with the Customer, who scans the QR code printed at their table to access the self-ordering interface on their own device. From there, the Customer browses the menu, adds the desired items to a cart, and proceeds to checkout, at which point the order is transmitted to the Cashier for confirmation.


Once the Cashier confirms the order, payment is collected in cash or through GCash, with the GCash reference number entered manually rather than verified through any payment gateway, consistent with the client's current payment collection process. A digital receipt is then generated and made available to the customer through the system rather than printed physically. At the same time the order is confirmed, it is relayed instantly to the Kitchen Display Queue, removing the verbal hand-off that the manual process in Figure 1 currently depends on.


        Simultaneously, this confirmation triggers the Menu Availability Engine. To determine the maximum number of dishes a customer can order, the engine divides the available raw stock by the ingredient quantities defined in the system’s recipe mapping. To ensure this capacity formula remains strictly accurate while active orders are being prepared, the system applies a “soft allocation” check. Ingredients required for pending orders in the Kitchen Display Queue are temporarily subtracted from the available stock pool. The self-ordering interface updates in real time based on this net availability, dynamically capping input limits or dimming dishes as “Sold Out” to prevent customers from ordering stock that is already committed to the kitchen. 


Kitchen Staff view the confirmed order on the Kitchen Display, prepare the dish, and mark the order served once it is ready. This single action is the point at which the system's intelligence layer begins: marking an order served automatically triggers a recipe-based deduction, which records the exact ingredient quantities consumed in the Stock Movement Ledger.


The Analytics Microservice continuously reads from this ledger to produce four outputs: a Demand Forecast projecting future ingredient needs, a Menu Engineering classification of dish profitability, a Spoilage Risk score for perishable stock, and a composite Business Health Score summarizing the day's overall performance. These outputs are consolidated on the Admin Dashboard, which the Admin reviews directly, and can also be queried in plain language through the Smart Assistant.


Underlying every stage of this process is real-time synchronization through WebSocket communication: the moment the Cashier confirms an order, the Kitchen Display Queue, the Admin Dashboard, and the Customer's own order tracking screen all update instantly, without any of the four roles needing to refresh their screen or ask another person for a status.


This proposed process directly replaces the manual sequence described in Section 4.1 and shown in Figure 1, where orders are taken verbally, billing is computed by hand, kitchen instructions are relayed by word of mouth, and ingredient stock is estimated by sight on no fixed schedule. Where the manual process leaves no record connecting what was sold to what was consumed, the proposed process generates that record automatically at the exact moment of service, and immediately puts it to use in the forecasting, profitability, and business health insights the owner receives each day.

Figure 5. Architectural Framework
  



The architectural framework of the system, illustrated in Figure 5, was designed around a clear separation between transactional processing and analytical computation, so that everyday order handling is never slowed down by forecasting or reporting workloads running alongside it. The entire system is deployed on Desi Zaika Snack House's own on-site infrastructure rather than a cloud platform, consistent with the restaurant's single-location scale of operation. The framework is organized into four user-facing modules built around a central backend, a local database, and an independent analytics service.


The Customer module begins when a customer scans the QR code printed at their table, which opens a self-ordering interface built with JavaScript and Tailwind CSS directly in their own device's browser. From there, the customer browses the menu, places an order, and is shown a live order-tracking screen that updates automatically once the order moves through the kitchen.


The Restaurant Floor (Cashier) module handles order confirmation and payment. The cashier confirms the incoming order on the POS screen and records payment in cash or via GCash, with the GCash reference number entered manually. A digital receipt is generated in place of a printed copy, and the confirmed order is relayed instantly to the kitchen queue.


The Kitchen module receives confirmed orders on the Kitchen Display, where kitchen staff prepare each dish and mark it served once ready. Marking an order served is the trigger point for the system's inventory intelligence: it initiates a recipe-based deduction that records exactly which ingredients, and how much of each, were consumed.


The Back Office (Admin) module gives the owner a device-based Admin Dashboard summarizing daily operations, along with a Smart Assistant that allows reports to be queried in plain language. This module surfaces four outputs generated by the analytics layer: demand forecasts, menu-engineering classifications, spoilage-risk scores, and a composite business health score.


All four modules communicate with the Laravel Backend, which serves as the central hub for authentication, role-based access control, and core business logic, backed by a single on-site PostgreSQL database that stores menu, recipe, order, payment, and stock-movement records. Because stock movements are recorded as an append-only ledger, an ingredient's current quantity is always derived from its movement history rather than stored as a single editable value, preserving a complete, auditable trail from a served dish back to the ingredients it consumed.


Real-time synchronization across all four modules is maintained by Laravel Reverb over WebSocket, which broadcasts every order confirmation, status change, and stock movement the instant it occurs. This is what keeps the customer's tracking screen, the kitchen queue, and the admin dashboard updated simultaneously without any role needing to refresh their screen or ask another person for a status.


Running independently of the Laravel backend is the Analytics Microservice, built with FastAPI (Python). It reads directly from the PostgreSQL database to compute demand forecasting, menu-engineering classification, spoilage-risk scoring, and the business health score, and consolidates these outputs on the Admin Dashboard. Keeping this service separate from the transactional backend ensures that a heavier analytical computation, such as recalculating a forecast, cannot introduce latency into an order or payment already in progress.


4.2 Analysis and Design
Figure 6. Entity Relationship Diagram (ERD)
  



        Figure 6 presents the Entity-Relationship Diagram for the proposed system. A single USERS entity holds authentication records for the Admin and Cashier roles, referenced by ORDERS through a foreign key that identifies who created or handled a given transaction. Each order in ORDERS is linked to one or more rows in ORDER_ITEMS, and each order item references a specific row in MENU_ITEMS, representing a dish sold and its quantity. To preserve historical financial integrity, the ORDER_ITEMS table includes historical_price and historical_cost columns, creating a static snapshot of the transaction to ensure that future recipe or price changes do not retroactively alter past profit analytics.  
Every order also produces exactly one row in PAYMENTS, capturing the payment method, GCash reference number where applicable, amount tendered, and change due.


        The recipe-level structure of the system is captured through the RECIPE_INGREDIENTS table, which links each MENU_ITEMS row to the specific INGREDIENTS it consumes and the quantity per serving. This is the table the system reads whenever an order is marked served: it determines exactly which rows in STOCK_MOVEMENTS to create, one per ingredient consumed, recording the quantity changed, the movement type, and a timestamp. Because STOCK_MOVEMENTS is append-only, the current quantity of any ingredient in INGREDIENTS is always derived by summing its movement history rather than being stored as a single mutable value, giving the system a complete and auditable trail from a served dish back to the exact ingredients it consumed. To also properly handle menu versioning, the RECIPE_INGREDIENTS table utilizes an is_active flag to archive older recipe iterations rather than overwriting them, guaranteeing that past ingredient consumption data remains mathematically accurate when a dish’s composition is updated.






4.3 Development Model
Figure 7. Iterative Waterfall Model (adapted from Jalote, 2005).
  



The study adopts an Iterative Waterfall Model, as described by Jalote (2005), as its software development model. As shown in Figure 7, the model proceeds through eight phases such as the System Feasibility, Requirements Analysis and Project Planning, System Design, Detailed Design, Coding, Testing and Integration, Installation, and Operations and Maintenance, with each phase's output document validated or verified before it is passed to the next phase. Unlike the classical waterfall model, where a phase is signed off and never revisited, this validation/verification step allows the team to rework a phase's output internally if it does not yet meet requirements, before that document moves forward. This suited the constraints under which the four-member team operated: a fixed capstone timeline, a small group with no dedicated project manager, and a client whose operational details, specific ingredient units of measurement, edge cases in how add-ons affect inventory, or adjustments to the Business Health Score's weighting, could only be fully understood through iterative consultation rather than a single upfront requirements-gathering session.


Development began with a feasibility assessment of the proposed system against the client's operations and the team's resources, producing the basis for the Requirements Analysis and Project Planning phase, where client interviews established the menu, recipe compositions, and functional needs, culminating in the requirements document and project plan. System Design followed, producing the conceptual framework and system architecture, while Detailed Design produced the ER diagram and use case diagram. Coding then proceeded module by module following the bottom-up approach, with the resulting programs verified during Testing and Integration to confirm that the interdependencies between the POS, inventory, and kitchen display components behaved as intended. Installation covered deployment and pilot use at Desi Zaika Snack House, after which the system entered Operations and Maintenance.












4.4 Development Approach 
Figure 8. Bottom-Up Development Approach


  



        The project utilizes a bottom-up development approach to construct the system's two-service architecture. Development initiates at the foundational data level by establishing the core database schemas in PostgreSQL, specifically focusing on the RECIPE_INGREDIENTS database relationships and the append-only stock movement ledger.


        While Figure 8 illustrates the system’s structural hierarchy from a top down perspective, breaking the integrated system down into its distinct modules and sub-module components, the actual implementation follows a strict bottom-up sequence. Once this data layer is stable, development progresses upward to build the independent backend services: the Laravel transactional operations and the Python FastAPI analytical computations. After these core microservices and WebSocket communication protocols are successfully integrated and tested, the highest-level components- the frontend user interfaces for the Admin Dashboards, POS, Kitchen Display System, and Customer Self-Ordering interface are developed and connected to the underlying APIs. This approach ensures that the complex data integrity rules and automated inventory deduction algorithms are fully functional before the user-facing elements are implemented.

4.7 Verification, Validation, and Testing 


Verification. To verify that the system is being developed correctly and aligns with the defined architectural specifications, evaluation is conducted systematically following the implementation phase of the Waterfall model. The system undergoes a formal structural review to ensure the two-service architecture effectively separates the transactional Laravel operations from the Python FastAPI analytics microservice without bottlenecking, strictly adhering to the initial Software Requirements Specification (SRS) defined during the planning phase.


Testing. To test that the system functions correctly without bugs or errors, formal testing protocols are executed during the dedicated testing phase of the Waterfall cycle. The development team employs Black Box Testing to evaluate the frontend functionality of the user interfaces, specifically the POS, Customer QR Ordering, and Admin Dashboard, ensuring all inputs produce the expected outputs without requiring knowledge of the internal code structures. Furthermore, integration testing is conducted to rigorously test the complex interdependencies between modules, specifically ensuring that a completed Point of Sale (POS) transaction accurately triggers recipe-based inventory deductions (backflushing) and that WebSocket events update the Kitchen Display System (KDS) and Admin Dashboard instantly. 


Validation.  To validate that the right system was built to solve the manual operation gaps of the client, the project utilizes User Acceptance Testing (UAT) during the deployment phase. The fully developed system will be deployed and demonstrated to the owner, staff, and customers of Desi Zaika Snack House for direct feedback.


Validation involves four respondent groups so that every module of the system is evaluated by the people who will actually use it: the owner/admin, who evaluates the Admin Dashboard and analytics layer as the system’s primary decision-maker; the cashier(s), who evaluate the POS and order-confirmation workflow; the kitchen staff, who evaluate the Kitchen Display System; and a sample of walk-in customers, who evaluate the self-ordering interface, since they are its direct end-users and the module cannot be meaningfully validated without their input.


The owner, cashier(s), and kitchen staff are included in full given the small size of Desi Zaika Snack House’s current staff complement: 1 owner, 1 cashier, and 2 kitchen staff.

The instrument will utilize a 5-point Likert scale to evaluate the system across four primary ISO/IEC 25010 characteristics relevant to the operational scope: Functional Suitability (e.g., accuracy of recipe-based stock deductions), Usability (e.g., ease of navigating the POS and Smart Assistant), Performance Efficiency (e.g., responsiveness of WebSocket real-time updates), and Reliability (e.g., system stability during transactions). The collected survey data will be analyzed using weighted means to determine the statistical acceptability and overall effectiveness of the system. 


        Compliance with Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR) Regulations.
Any point-of-sale system deployed for a business operating in the Philippines needs to account for Bureau of Internal Revenue (BIR) requirements, since software that has not been registered or built with these requirements in mind can leave a business exposed during a tax audit. Securing formal BIR accreditation was not attempted within this study's timeline, but four aspects of the system's design were shaped with that eventual requirement in view:


1. Itemized Digital Receipts
Each completed transaction produces a digital receipt covering the information a compliant invoice would need to show:
* Business name and address
* Order ID and the date and time of the transaction
* An itemized list of what was ordered and its price
* The payment method used


2. Audit Trail and Transaction Logs
Every sale is recorded the moment it happens, tagged with a timestamp and the ID of the cashier who processed it. Once logged, a transaction record cannot afterward be altered or removed, which keeps the sales history reliable for anyone reviewing it later.


3. Centralized, Access-Controlled Storage
Rather than sitting in spreadsheets or local files a staff member could quietly edit, transaction records live in the system's PostgreSQL database, where changes are governed by the same role-based access control as the rest of the platform.


4. A Foundation for Later Accreditation
The system has not gone through the BIR's Computerized Accounting System (CAS) certification process, a step that reflects the capstone team's timeframe and Desi Zaika Snack House's current scale as a single-location business rather than a limitation in the design itself. Because the receipt format, logging behavior, and storage approach were already built around these principles, pursuing accreditation later would mean seeking certification for what exists, not restructuring the system to qualify for it.
Taken together, these choices stop short of making the system BIR-certified, but they give Desi Zaika Snack House financial records that hold up to scrutiny,  which matters for compliance and for running the business well, independent of whether certification is ever pursued.
